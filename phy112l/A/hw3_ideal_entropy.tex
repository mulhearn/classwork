In this assignment, you will introduce the Gibbs Entropy as a

\section{Gibbs Entropy}

The entropy of a macrostate is defined as:
\begin{equation}
S = k \ln \Omega
\end{equation}
where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant and $\Omega$ is the multiplicity of
microstates corresponding to the macrostate.

To compute the Gibbs Entropy, we divide the total phase space into $M$
cells.  The macrostate is specified by the number of particles in each
cell $n_i$.  To count these microstates, start by assuming that we have
only two cells, with the first containing $a$ particles.  The binomial
theorem gives us the multiplicity of states:
$$\Omega_{(2)} = \frac{N!}{a! (N-a)!}$$
If we divide the second cell into two cells containing $b$ and $c$ particles respectively, with:
$$N-a = b+c$$
then the binomial theorem implies that the multiplicity of states is:
$$\Omega_{(3)} = \frac{N!}{a! (N-a)!} \cdot \frac{(N-a)!}{b! c!} = \frac{N!}{a! b! c!}$$
If we continue in this way for $M$ cells, we arrive at the multinomial coefficient:
\begin{equation}
\Omega = \frac{N!}{\prod_{i=1}^M n_i!}
\end{equation}
Calculating
$$\ln \Omega = \ln N! - \sum_i \ln n_i!$$
and applying Stirling's approximation:

$$\ln \Omega =  N \ln N - N - \sum_i (n_i \ln n_i - n_i)$$
Using $\sum_i n_i = N$ we expand the sum and cancel terms:
\[
  \ln\Omega = N\ln N - N - \sum_i n_i\ln n_i + \sum_i n_i
           = N\ln N - \sum_i n_i\ln n_i,
\]
and hence
\[
  \ln\Omega = -\sum_i n_i\big(\ln n_i - \ln N\big)
             = -\sum_i n_i\ln\!\left(\frac{n_i}{N}\right).
\]
and so:
$$\frac{S}{kN} = - \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N} \ln \left( \frac{n_i}{N} \right) $$
and defining probability per bin as:
$$p_i = \frac{n_i}{N}$$
we have at last:
\begin{equation}
\frac{S}{kN} = - \sum_i p_i \ln ( p_i )
\end{equation}
which is known as the Gibbs Entropy (or Shannon Entropy for $k$=1).
When calculating the Gibbs Entropy numerically, set the contribution
from bins with $p_i=0$ to zero, which is consistent with the limiting
behavior.

For a uniform distribution in $M$ cells, the probability of visiting each cell is identical, with:
$$p_i = 1/M$$
and so in this case:
\begin{equation}
\frac{S}{kN} = \ln ( M )
\end{equation}
Notice that even in this simple case, the Gibbs Entropy is dependent
on the choice of bins.

As is always the case for classical entropy, the Gibbs entropy is only
determined to within an overall constant, which in this case depends
on the cells that we have chosen.  However, as long as we keep our
choice of bins constant, we can reliably calculate a change in entropy
between initial and final states using this formula.  To compute
$p_i$, we need only count the number of particles present in each cell
and divide by the total number of particles.

To determine the values of $p_i$ more reliably (with more statistics)
we can simulate many time steps, and count the number of visits of
particles to each cell.  Dividing the result by the total number of
visits (the number of particles times the number of time steps) we
determine $p_i$ in each cell with less statistical uncertainty.  This
only works once the system has reached equilibrium (or else the Gibbs
Entropy will be changing).  With the function {\tt gibbs\_entropy}
below, you do not need to count the visits yourself: save the arrays
{\tt vx}, {\tt vy} and {\tt y} at each time step you want to
include, join each of them into one long array with {\tt
  np.concatenate}, and pass the three long arrays to {\tt
  gibbs\_entropy}.  The number of entries is then the number of visits.

\section{Entropy Changes for an Ideal Gas}

If no work is done on our gas, then the heat added equals the change in internal energy,
$$\Delta U = Q.$$
For a reversible heating process the entropy change is
$$\mathrm{d}S = \frac{\delta Q_{\rm rev}}{T}.$$
At constant volume (no work), $\delta Q_{\rm rev} = \mathrm{d}U$, and for a 2-D ideal gas $U = NkT$, so $\mathrm{d}U = Nk\,\mathrm{d}T$. Hence
\[
\mathrm{d}S = \frac{Nk\,\mathrm{d}T}{T},
\]
and integrating between initial and final temperatures gives
\[
\Delta S = \int_{T_i}^{T_f} \frac{Nk\,\mathrm{d}T}{T} = Nk\ln\!\left(\frac{T_f}{T_i}\right).
\]
Next, suppose that instead that we expand the gas from $V_1$ to $V_2$
at constant temperature.  The velocity profile remains unchanged, due
to the constant temperature, and so the phase-space only changes by
the volume, with:
$$\Omega \propto V^N$$
and so:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \Delta S &=& k \, \ln(\Omega_2) - k \, \ln(\Omega_1) \\
           &=& k \ln\left(\frac{\Omega_2}{\Omega_1}\right) \\
\end{eqnarray*}
and so:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S = N \,k \, \ln \,\left( \frac{V_2}{V_1} \right)
\end{equation}

\section{Ideal Gas with a Step Potential}

Consider an ideal gas contained within $0 \leq y \leq 4 a$ subject to a potential energy:
\begin{equation}
  V(y) = \begin{cases}
    V_{\rm step} & y > 2 a \\
    0 & {\rm otherwise} \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
We can develop an accurate numerical simulation for this potential
based on conservation of energy, but with the crucial caveat that the
molecules' momentum in the $x$ direction is unchanged by the potential.
We define the contribution to the kinetic energy due to the particles'
motion in the $y$-direction as
\begin{equation}
  K_{(y)} \equiv \frac{1}{2} m v_y^2
\end{equation}
Molecules in the lower region ($y<2a$) with:
$$ K_{(y)} \le V_{\rm step} $$
never escape the lower region, and bounce at the boundaries.  But molecules with
$$ K_{(y)} > V_{\rm step} $$
can cross from the lower region to the upper region ($y>2a$), and have the
momentum in the $y$ direction reduced such that:
$$ K_{(y)} \to K_{(y)} - V_{\rm step} $$
Molecules can always cross from the upper region into the lower
region, and have their momentum in the $y$ direction increased such
that:
$$ K_{(y)} \to K_{(y)} + V_{\rm step} $$
We will call the function that implements this algorithm {\tt reflect}.

For a gas in equilibrium at temperature $T$ (kept constant by
thermal contact with a reservoir), the relative occupancy of the upper
and lower regions (which are the same area) will be:
$$\frac{p_{\rm up}}{p_{\rm dn}} = \exp \left( - \frac{V_{\rm step}}{kT} \right) \equiv \alpha $$
and so:
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm up} = \frac{\alpha}{1 + \alpha}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
p_{\rm dn} = \frac{1}{1 + \alpha}
\end{equation}
If we consider this like a two state system going from the ground state ($S=0$) to a state at equilibrium with occupancies $p_{\rm up}$ and $p_{\rm dn}$, we expect the change in entropy to be:
$$\frac{\Delta S}{kN} = - (p_{\rm up} \ln p_{\rm up} + p_{\rm dn} \ln p_{\rm dn})$$


\section{System of Units}

We will use the same system of units as the one we use for the first
assignment (as there is no longer any need to add a constraint for
gravity, which we will not consider in this assignment).  We assume
there is some relevant length scale $a$ which we set to $1$ and a
relevant temperature $T_0$ which we set to $1$ as well.

The other units that we defined in the previous homework continue to
apply, so let's recall them here.  Motivated by the Maxwell-Boltzmann
distribution, the velocity unit $v_0$ is defined by:
$$ v_0^2 \equiv \frac{k T_0}{m}$$
and the unit for time is:
$$ \tau \equiv a / v_0$$
Motivated by the ideal gas law, we can set the unit of total energy to:
$$U_0 = N k T_0$$
For this assignment, you will measure the potential energy (of a
single particle) using units of
$$V_0 = k T_0$$
Remember that for analytic work, we keep these factors in place.  But when using an
analytic result within our computer programs, we set all of these quantities to 1:
$$m = a = T_0 = v_0 = \tau = U_0 = V_0 = 1$$
As before, the temperature of an ideal gas is still calculated through
the average kinetic energy, which in our system of units becomes:
\begin{equation}
 \label{eqn:expv}
  \frac{T}{T_0} = \frac{\braket{v_x^2/v_0^2} + \braket{v_y^2/v_0^2}}{2}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eqn:expv} gives the dimensionless temperature, which
in our units is also equal to the total kinetic energy of the gas
divided by $U_0$:
\[
\frac{U_{\rm kinetic}}{U_0} = \frac{T}{T_0}.
\]
(Equivalently, this is the average energy per particle, in dimensionless units.)
