\section{Fourier Series for Complex-Valued Functions}

We will treat the Fourier Series in the context of the inner product space of square-integrable periodic functions of period $a$,  with an inner product defined as:
$$ \braket{f|g} \coloneqq \int_{-\frac{a}{2}}^{\frac{a}{2}} f^*(x) g(x) dx$$
The complex exponential functions
$$e_k(x) \coloneqq \sqrt{\frac{1}{a}} \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi k x}{a}\right)$$
are orthonormal vectors.  To verify:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \braket{e_j|e_k} &=& \frac{1}{a} \int_{-\frac{a}{2}}^{\frac{a}{2}}
  \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi (k-j) x}{a}\right) dx \\[7pt]
  &=& \frac{1}{\pi (k-j)} \cdot \frac{\exp\left(i \pi (k-j) \right)-\exp\left(-i \pi (k-j) \right)}{2i}\\[7pt]
  &=& \sinc(\pi (k-j))
\end{eqnarray*}
As $k$ and $j$ are integers, and $\sinc$ is zero for every integer multiple of $\pi$ except for $\sinc(0)=1$, we have shown:
\begin{equation}
  \braket{e_j|e_k} = \delta_{kj}
\end{equation}
We will not derive it here, but the complex functions are also a complete basis, so we can expand any function in terms of the basis of complex exponentials by:
\begin{equation}
 \psi(x) = \sum_{j=-\infty}^{+\infty} c_j e_j (x)
\end{equation}
Because of othonormality, we can determine the Fourier coefficients by:
\begin{equation}
c_k = \braket{e_k | \psi}
\end{equation}

\section{Discrete Fourier Transform}

Next we want to consider the case where we only know the value of $\psi(x)$ at $n$ discrete positions of $x$, specifically:
$$x_j = x_0 + \frac{a}{n} j$$
for $x_0=-a/2$ and $j=0,1,\ldots,n-1$.
Notice that the sequence ends before $x=a/2$ because this point is redundant to $x=-a/2$ for the periodic functions that we are considering.

We account for the discrete sampling by making the replacement:
$$\psi(x) \to \frac{a}{n} \, \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \, \delta(x-x_j)  \; \psi(x)$$
which is choosen because:
$$\int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{a}{n} \, \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \, \delta(x-x_j)  \, \psi(x) \, dx = \frac{a}{n} \sum_{m=m_1}^{m_2}  \,\psi_m \; \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} \; \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \, \psi(x) \, dx $$
showing that integration (smoothing) of discrete sampled function
reproduces the integral of the original function as the space between
samples approaches zero.  We do expect this sampling to introduce
high-frequency artifacts which must be considered, which we will
discuss later.

Calculating the fourier series coefficients with the sampled function we obtain:\begin{eqnarray*}
  c_k &=& \braket{e_k | \psi} \\[5pt]
  &=& \sqrt{\frac{1}{a}} \int_{-\frac{a}{2}}^{\frac{a}{2}}
  \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi k x}{a}\right) \, \frac{a}{n} \, \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} \delta(x-x_j) \, \psi(x) \, dx \\[7pt]
  &=& \frac{\sqrt{a}}{n} \; \sum_{m=0}^{n-1}
  \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi m k}{n}\right) \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi k x_0}{a}\right) \, \psi_j \, dx
\end{eqnarray*}
which we can reorganize as:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{c_k}{\sqrt{a/n}} \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi k x_0}{a}\right)
 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{m=0}^{n-1}
  \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi m k}{n}\right) \psi_m \equiv A_k  
\end{equation}
where $A_k$ are the Fourier coefficients from {\tt numpy.fft.fft} if called with the normalization option {\tt norm=``ortho''}.  The complex exponential on the left hand side is a phase factor that accounts the location of the first data point $x_0$.  Notice that the right hand side depends only on indices, not the actual $x$ positions.

\begin{figure}[thb]
\begin{center}
{\includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{plots/trouble.pdf}}
\end{center}
\caption{\label{fig:trouble} Trouble.}
\end{figure}

Something very interesting has happened to our Fourier coefficients as
a direct result of discrete sampling.  Notice that:
$$A_{k+n} = A_{k}$$
because the phase shift introducted in each term in the sum above is:
$$exp(-i 2 \pi m) = 1$$
So evidently we have only $n$ unique Fourier coefficients, at which point they repeat themselves.

Something very interesting is going on here, and we can build an
intuitive picture from Fig.~\ref{fig:trouble}.  We see that signal
contributions for wavelengths:
$$\lambda \leq 2\frac{a}{n}$$
are potentially problematic, as they can be undetectable.
In terms of the index $k$ in each $c_k$, this becomes:
$$\frac{a}{|k|} = \lambda < 2\frac{a}{n}$$
or
$$|k| < 2/n$$
For odd numbers of $n$ this is an unambiguous prescription to avoid trouble:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  n=1 &\implies& k = 0 \\
  n=3 &\implies& k = -1,0,1 \\
  n=5 &\implies& k = -2,-1,0,1,2 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
We see that we have one unique coefficient available for each allowed
wave number.  We simply set the remaining coefficients to zero.  If we
know that our signal has no contributions from those shorter
wavelengths then our Fourier series will be complete and accurate.

For odd number of $n$ the situation is a bit more complicated.  If we stick to strictly less than for the allowed $k$ values, we have:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  n=2 &\implies& k = 0 \\
  n=4 &\implies& k = -1,0,1 \\
  n=6 &\implies& k = -2,-1,0,1,2 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
which does not use our unique Fourier coefficients.

A completely reasonable and practical thing to do in the situation would be too say, find, throw out the shorter wavelength coefficients.  We'll just keep our sampling rate high enough that all of these coefficients are zero.  This is reasonable, and totally works.

The only downside is that it makes the DFT a lossy operation.  We cannot get back the original sampled data back exactly from the transform.  So it's worth tyring to fix things up.  Look up what happens  if we loosen the requirements to on $k$ to less than or equal:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  n=2 &\implies& k = -1,0,1 \\
  n=4 &\implies& k = -2,-1,0,1,2 \\
  n=6 &\implies& k = -3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
which would require one more Fourier coefficient than we have.  What's
going on?

\begin{figure}[thb]
\begin{center}
{\includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{plots/notrouble.pdf}}
\end{center}
\caption{\label{fig:notrouble} No trouble.}
\end{figure}

As can be seen from Fig~\ref{fig:notrouble} some phases can be
sampled, even at the limit, but not all.  So we need to know something
about the the contribution at the limit: it's purely real and even,
for example.  In practice, we make the assumption that makes the
inverse discrete Fourier transform the easiest to write down (just
drop the $c_{n/2}$ factor), and make the allowed $k$ values:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  n=2 &\implies& k = -1,0 \\
  n=4 &\implies& k = -2,-1,0,1 \\  
  n=6 &\implies& k = -3,-2,-1,0,1,2 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
This puts one term in the series for each unique coeefficent, and
makes the DFT lossless.

This book keeping is conveniently handled for you by python with the
{\tt numpy.fft.fftfreq} function, which returns an array the same size
as the Fourier coefficients that reports $a/k$.  For equation purposes we will note the set of allowed $k$ values as $K(n)$.

We can reconstitute our original wave function (wihtout any artifacts from sampling) as:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \psi(x) &=& \sum_{k \in K(n)} c_k e_k (x) \\
  &=& \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\sum_{k \in K(n)}  c_k \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi k x}{a}\right) \\
  &=& \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=j_a}^{j_b} A_k  \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi k (x-x_0)}{a}\right) \\  
\end{eqnarray*}
