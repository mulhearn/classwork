\section{Notation}

We'll reserve $k$ for the wave number of a particle.  We will use $m$
and $n$ as dummy indices.  So we will use $M$ (capital) for the mass
of a particle to avoid confusion with the index $m$.  We'll use $N$
for the number of samples.  We'll use $a$ for the period of a
function.  Note that the numpy FFT library uses different notation,
taking $k$ as the index for Fourier coefficients (our $m$) and $n$ for
the number of samples (our $N$).

\section{Fourier Series for Complex-Valued Functions}

We will treat the Fourier Series in the context of the inner product
space of square-integrable periodic functions of period $a$, with an
inner product defined as:
$$ \braket{f|g} \coloneqq \int_{-\frac{a}{2}}^{\frac{a}{2}} f^*(x) g(x) dx$$
The complex exponential functions
$$e_m(x) \coloneqq \sqrt{\frac{1}{a}} \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi k x}{a}\right)$$
are orthonormal vectors.  To verify:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \braket{e_m|e_n} &=& \frac{1}{a} \int_{-\frac{a}{2}}^{\frac{a}{2}}
  \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi (n-m) x}{a}\right) dx \\[7pt]
  &=& \frac{1}{\pi (n-m)} \cdot \frac{\exp\left(i \pi (n-m) \right)-\exp\left(-i \pi (n-m) \right)}{2i}\\[7pt]
  &=& \sinc(\pi (n-m))
\end{eqnarray*}
As $n$ and $m$ are integers, and $\sinc$ is zero for every integer multiple of $\pi$ except for $\sinc(0)=1$, we have shown:
\begin{equation}
  \braket{e_m|e_n} = \delta_{mn}
\end{equation}
We will not derive it here, but the complex functions are also a complete basis, so we can expand any function in terms of the basis of complex exponentials by:
\begin{equation}
 \psi(x) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty} c_m e_m (x)
\end{equation}
Because of othonormality, we can determine the Fourier coefficients by:
\begin{equation}
c_m = \braket{e_m | \psi}
\end{equation}

\section{Discrete Fourier Transform}

Next we want to consider the case where we only know the value of $\psi(x)$ at $N$ discrete positions of $x$, specifically:
$$x_m = x_0 + m \, \Delta a$$
where
$$\Delta a \coloneqq \frac{a}{N}$$
for\footnote{You might wonder why not just put $x_0=0$, and indeed,
that would work fine for perioidic functions.  However, choosing $x_0
= -a/2$ will be much more convenenient later when we are dealing with
potentials centered at zero.}
$$x_0=-\frac{a}{2}$$
and
$$m=0,1,\ldots,N-1$$
Notice that {\bf the sequence does not include} $\mathbf{a/2}$ because this point is redundant to $x_0=-a/2$ for the periodic functions that we are
considering.

We account for the discrete sampling by making the replacement:
$$\psi(x) \to \frac{a}{N} \, \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} \, \delta(x-x_m)  \; \psi(x)$$
which is choosen because:
$$\int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{a}{N} \, \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} \, \delta(x-x_m)  \, \psi(x) \, dx = \frac{a}{N} \sum_{m=m_1}^{m_2}  \,\psi_m \; \xrightarrow[N \to \infty]{} \; \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \, \psi(x) \, dx $$
showing that integration (smoothing) of discrete sampled function
reproduces the integral of the original function as the space between
samples approaches zero.  We do expect this sampling to introduce
high-frequency artifacts which must be considered, which we will
discuss later.

Calculating the Fourier series coefficients with the sampled function we obtain:\begin{eqnarray*}
  c_m &=& \braket{e_m | \psi} \\[5pt]
  &=& \sqrt{\frac{1}{a}} \int_{-\frac{a}{2}}^{\frac{a}{2}}
  \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi m x}{a}\right) \, \frac{a}{N} \, \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \delta(x-x_n) \, \psi(x) \, dx \\[7pt]
  &=& \frac{\sqrt{a}}{N} \; \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
  \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi m n}{N}\right) \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi n x_0}{a}\right) \, \psi_n \, dx
\end{eqnarray*}
which we can reorganize as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:cm}
  \frac{c_m}{\sqrt{a/N}} \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi m x_0}{a}\right)
 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{n=0}^{n-1}
  \exp\left(-i \frac{2\pi m n}{N}\right) \psi_m 
\end{equation}
Notice that the right hand side depends only on indices, not the
actual $x$ positions.  This is an important insight.  The FFT takes
function values to coefficients, never knowing the actual $x$
positions.  If you checkout the documentation for {\tt numpy.fft.fft},
you can confirm that the right hand side of that equation is Fourier
coefficient $A_m$ as provided by the tool with {\bf the normalization
  option {\tt norm=``ortho''}} So when moving between the coefficients
provided by the tool and our derivations, there are some extra factors
involved:
$$A_m \equiv \frac{c_m}{\sqrt{a/N}} \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi m x_0}{a}\right)$$
The complex exponential is a phase factor that accounts the location of the first data point $x_0$, which we are taking as $-a/2$.  We can work with our $c_m$ coefficients, but we'll have to account for these additional factors once we are using the library tools.

\begin{figure}[thb]
\begin{center}
{\includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{plots/trouble.pdf}}
\end{center}
\caption{\label{fig:trouble} Trouble.}
\end{figure}

Something very interesting has happened to our Fourier coefficients as
a direct result of discrete sampling.  Have a look at Equation~\ref{eqn:cm}
and notice that:
$$c_{m+N} = c_{m}$$
because the phase shift introduced in each term in the sum above is:
$$\exp(-i 2 \pi N) = 1$$
So evidently we have only $N$ unique Fourier coefficients, at which point they repeat themselves.

We can build an intuitive picture of what is going on from Fig.~\ref{fig:trouble}.  We see that signal
contributions for wavelengths:
$$\lambda \leq 2\frac{a}{N}$$
are potentially problematic, as they can be undetectable.
In terms of the index $m$ in each $c_m$, this becomes:
$$\frac{a}{|m|} = \lambda < 2\frac{a}{N}$$
or
$$|m| < 2/N$$
For odd numbers of $N$ this is an unambiguous prescription to avoid trouble:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  N=1 &\implies& m = 0 \\
  N=3 &\implies& m = -1,0,1 \\
  N=5 &\implies& m = -2,-1,0,1,2 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
We see that we have one unique coefficient available for each allowed
wave number.  We simply set the remaining coefficients to zero.  If we
know that our signal has no contributions from those shorter
wavelengths then our Fourier series will be complete and accurate.

For even numbers of $N$ the situation is a bit more complicated.  If we stick to strictly less than for the allowed $m$ values, we have:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  N=2 &\implies& m = 0 \\
  N=4 &\implies& m = -1,0,1 \\
  N=6 &\implies& m = -2,-1,0,1,2 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
which does not use all of our unique Fourier coefficients.

A completely reasonable and practical thing to do in the situation
would be too say ``Fine! Throw out the shorter wavelength coefficients.
We'll just keep our sampling rate high enough that all of these
coefficients are zero.''  This is reasonable, and totally works.
The only downside is that it makes the DFT a lossy operation.  We
cannot get back the original sampled data back exactly from the
transform.  So it's worth tyring to fix things up.  Look at what
happens if we loosen the requirements to on $m$ to less than or equal:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  N=2 &\implies& m = -1,0,1 \\
  N=4 &\implies& m = -2,-1,0,1,2 \\
  N=6 &\implies& m = -3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
which would require one more Fourier coefficient than we have.  What's
going on?

\begin{figure}[thb]
\begin{center}
{\includegraphics[width=0.60\textwidth]{plots/notrouble.pdf}}
\end{center}
\caption{\label{fig:notrouble} No trouble.}
\end{figure}

As can be seen from Fig~\ref{fig:notrouble} some phases can be
sampled, even at the limit, but not all.  So we need to know something
about the the contribution at the limit: if it's purely real and even,
for example.  In practice, we make the assumption that makes the
inverse discrete Fourier transform the easiest to write down (just
drop the $c_{n/2}$ factor), and make the allowed $m$ values:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  N=2 &\implies& m = -1,0 \\
  N=4 &\implies& m = -2,-1,0,1 \\  
  N=6 &\implies& m = -3,-2,-1,0,1,2 \\
\end{eqnarray*}
This puts one term in the series for each unique coefficient, and
makes the DFT lossless.

This book keeping is conveniently handled for you by python with the
{\tt numpy.fft.fftfreq} function, which returns an array the same size
as the Fourier coefficients that reports $m/n$.  For equation purposes we will note the set of allowed $m$ values as $M(N)$.

We can reconstitute our original wave function (wihtout any artifacts from sampling) as:
\begin{eqnarray*}
  \psi(x) &=& \sum_{m \in M(N)} c_m e_m (x) \\
  &=& \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\sum_{m \in M(N)}  c_m \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi k x}{a}\right) \\
  &=& \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{m \in M(N)} A_m  \exp\left(i \frac{2\pi k (x-x_0)}{a}\right) \\  
\end{eqnarray*}
